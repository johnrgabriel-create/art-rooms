Twelve Rooms of Painting

720 works in twelve rooms of 60. Four rooms follow European painting from 1400 to 1900. The East Asia wing holds a thousand years of Chinese painting and calligraphy, twelve centuries of Japanese art, and fifteen centuries of Korean art. The Islamic World wing holds twelve centuries of art from Spain and West Africa to Iran, Central Asia and Indonesia. The South Asia wing holds more than two thousand years of art from India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal and Tibet. The Southeast Asia wing holds nearly three thousand years of art from Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, Vietnam, Malaysia, Indonesia, Timor-Leste and the Philippines. The Africa wing holds some thirty thousand years of art from across the continent, from the rock paintings of Namibia and the Sahara to Egypt and Nubia on the Nile, the kingdoms of West and Central Africa, Ethiopia, Great Zimbabwe and the Swahili coast. The Americas wing holds some three thousand years of art from Alaska to the Andes, from the Olmec and the Maya to the Inca, the Mexica, the peoples of North America and the painters of the colonial and independent Americas.

In the 1400s, the Renaissance brought a renewed interest in ancient Greece and Rome, the human figure, nature, and the world as it actually looked. Painters in Italy and Flanders set out to make a flat surface look like a window, using perspective, realism, and increasingly sophisticated oil paint. In the 1600s, many turned to drama, with deep shadows, strong light, movement, and emotion, while Dutch painters increasingly depicted ordinary rooms, people, landscapes, and everyday life. In the late 1700s and early 1800s, some painters returned to the calm order of ancient Greece and Rome, while others pursued emotion, imagination, and wild nature. By the late 1800s, painters had increasingly left the studio to paint modern life and changing light, and a picture no longer had to look polished or “finished.”

In China, painting and calligraphy were sister arts, made with the same brush and ink on silk or paper. Many of the greatest works are handscrolls, unrolled an arm’s length at a time from right to left, or hanging scrolls and album leaves. Landscape became the leading subject in the Song dynasty. Later owners added their seals and written comments, so a scroll often carries its own history.

In Japan, artists took in Buddhist painting and Chinese ink painting and made forms of their own. Court painters told long stories in illustrated handscrolls, Zen monks painted in ink, and from the late 1500s artists covered folding screens and sliding doors with gold leaf. Calligraphy, tea bowls and lacquer ranked alongside painting. In the 1700s and 1800s, color woodblock prints brought actors, beauties and landscapes to buyers in the cities.

In Korea, the oldest paintings are the walls of Goguryeo tombs from the 300s to the 600s. Goryeo painters made Buddhist paintings in gold and color, and Goryeo potters made celadon inlaid with white and black clay. In the Joseon dynasty, Jeong Seon painted Korea's own mountains as they looked, Kim Hong-do and Sin Yun-bok painted everyday life, and King Sejong gave Korean its own alphabet. Court screens stood behind the throne, and folk paintings of tigers, magpies and written characters hung in ordinary homes.

In the Islamic world, calligraphy was the highest art, because it carried the words of the Qur'an. Scribes wrote from right to left in Kufic, naskh, thuluth and nasta’liq, and the same writing ran across paper, tile, metal, glass and carpet. Mosques were lined with mosaic and tile, and domes were built up from small stacked niches called muqarnas. Court painters in Tabriz, Herat, Isfahan and Istanbul illustrated histories and poems, and collectors gathered paintings and calligraphy into albums.

In South Asia, rulers and donors paid for art as acts of piety and power, from the stone pillars of the Maurya emperor Ashoka in the 200s BCE to the carved stupas at Sanchi and the painted caves at Ajanta. Monks and merchants carried Buddhism and its images to Sri Lanka, Central Asia, China and Southeast Asia, and printed and dyed cotton from Gujarat and the Coromandel coast was traded from Egypt to Indonesia. From the 400s kings built stone temples where worshippers came for darshan, to see the image of the god and be seen by it, and from 1526 the Mughal emperors brought Persian painters to a court workshop whose portraits Rembrandt copied in Amsterdam in the late 1650s. In the late 1700s, as the East India Company took power in Bengal by war and by collecting its taxes, painters who had worked for Mughal and regional courts painted for Company officials instead. Works left by conquest, from Tipu Sultan’s tiger organ seized at Seringapatam in 1799 to the Koh-i-Noor diamond taken from the Sikh kingdom in 1849, and in 1879 the Company’s India Museum in London passed 19,228 objects to the museum now called the V&A.

In Southeast Asia, bronze drums and burial jars came long before writing. From the 600s, kings took in Hindu and Buddhist ideas from India and built temples of their own, from Borobudur in Java to Angkor Wat in Cambodia and the thousands of temples at Bagan in Myanmar. In Vietnam, the Đại Việt court took Buddhism and Chinese forms and made them its own. Women wove and dyed cloth such as batik, ikat and tais, and shadow puppets, lacquer and gold ranked alongside painting. In the 1800s, Raden Saleh in Java and Juan Luna in the Philippines took up European oil painting.

In Africa, people painted and carved on rock for tens of thousands of years. On the Nile, Egypt and the Nubian kingdom of Kush built pyramids and temples, and in the 700s BCE the kings of Kush conquered Egypt and ruled it as pharaohs. Brass casters at Ife and Benin made royal heads and plaques, Ethiopian painters filled Gospel books and church walls, and from the late 1400s carvers in Sierra Leone and Benin made ivory saltcellars for Portuguese buyers. The Atlantic slave trade carried millions of Africans to the Americas, and after the Berlin Conference of 1884–85 seven European powers took all of Africa but Ethiopia and Liberia by 1914. Picasso and other painters in Paris took forms from masks brought to France through colonial channels, while in 1928 Mahmoud Mokhtar unveiled Egypt’s Awakening in Cairo, paid for by public subscription after the 1919 revolution against British rule. Works left by conquest, from the Abomey treasury taken by French troops in 1892 to the Benin bronzes seized by British troops in 1897, and a 2018 report to the French president cited experts’ estimate that over 90 percent of sub-Saharan Africa’s heritage is held outside the continent; France returned 26 Abomey treasures in 2021 and Germany began returning Benin bronzes to Nigeria in 2022.

In the Americas, the Olmec carved colossal heads more than three thousand years ago, and the Maya painted, carved and wrote on temples, stelae and vases. In the Andes, weavers made cloth the most valued art, and the Inca built in fitted stone and kept records on knotted cords called khipu. Mixtec and Mexica painters made screenfold books, and feather workers made headdresses and mosaics. After the Spanish and Portuguese conquests, Indigenous and mestizo artists painted for churches in Cusco and Mexico City, and nations such as the Diné, Lakota, Tlingit and Haida kept weaving, beading and carving. Many works left their homes by conquest, excavation or seizure, from Moctezuma's headdress to the Kwakwaka'wakw regalia seized under Canada's potlatch ban in 1922.
