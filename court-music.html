Court Music Atlas

What is court music?

From Lewis Peterman's seminar, Court Music of Eurasia (SDSU, 2008)

The court

A court is a ruler's household that also runs the state. The course defines it as "an extended household centered on a patron." Wherever the ruler's household and the government overlap, "it is sensible to speak of a 'court'", whether in Ming China, Achaemenid Persia or Habsburg Vienna.

Courts paid for music, housed musicians and set the rules of taste. The essay ends with the line that explains why so much of this music survives: "Etiquette and hierarchy flourish in highly structured court settings and may leave conservative traces over generations."

Five threads that run through every region

  1. A mode and a cycle. Melody follows a mode (maqam, raga, the Javanese scales) and time follows a repeating cycle (iqa', tala, jangdan, the gong cycle).
  2. Free first, fixed after. An unmeasured solo opens the mode, then a composed piece follows: taqsim then peşrev, alap then gat, bughyah then mizan.
  3. The suite. Long chains of pieces in one mode: the Ottoman fasıl, the Arab wasla, the Iraqi maqam, the Moroccan nuba, the gagaku program.
  4. A ranked ensemble. Each instrument has a job. The gamelan divides into six layers, from the great gong that closes each cycle to the fast elaborating parts.
  5. Music as order. Court music stands for something: Confucian propriety in East Asia, the "dignity of the kingdom" in Korea, the ruler's praise and genealogy in West Africa.

How to listen

John Miller Chernoff, quoted in the course: "Many Non-Western musics are not just different music but are something that is different from 'music.'" The course asks why music is made and what it does, as well as how it is built.

Instruments throughout follow the four classes of the Hornbostel–Sachs system: chordophones (strings), aerophones (winds), membranophones (drums with skins) and idiophones (gongs, bells, xylophones, rattles).

Europe

In the art rooms: Renaissance · Baroque & Rococo · Neoclassical & Romantic · Realism & Impressionism

The big idea

European court music moved from a local, intimate art to a cosmopolitan profession. The course sets "early" music, before 1600, against "modern" music. Early music was "a strictly local expression," "softly played in intimate gatherings" by "variously trained amateurs." Modern music is loud, public, international and made by paid professionals.

Four eras

Middle Ages (13th–14th centuries). Texture moves from one line to several, and rhythm from repeating patterns to measured notation. Guillaume de Machaut (1300–1377) is the central court poet-composer.

Renaissance (15th–16th centuries). Burgundian and Netherlands composers set the style. Four or five imitative voices in a smooth, consonant sound: the Mass and motet for church, the chanson, Lied and madrigal for court. Instruments play in consorts of one family, "whole" (all viols) or "broken" (mixed). The Italian madrigal ran from 1525 to 1620 and ended with Monteverdi at Mantua.

Baroque (1600–1750). The continuo, a bass line with improvised chords, supports everything. Tonality settles around 1680. New forms: opera, oratorio, cantata, concerto, sonata, suite. Lully runs the music of Louis XIV at Versailles.

Enlightenment (1750–1800). Sonata form, the symphony and the string quartet. Equal temperament and the piano. Haydn serves the Esterházy princes; Mozart and Beethoven work in Vienna.

In the art rooms: Islamic World

The big idea

One family of classical music runs from Morocco to Uzbekistan, carried by conquest and court contact. The course names five schools: Turkish, Persian, Arabic (centred on Egypt), Central Asian and Andalusian (North Africa). Its goal is tarab, the "sustained elaboration of a single mood with modulations."

How the music works

Mode. A maqam (dastgah in Iran) is a scale with microtones, stock phrases, and a mood. The course lists:

Maqam Feeling
rast pride, power, soundness of mind
bayati vitality, joy
sikah love
saba sadness and pain
hijaz the distant desert

Rhythm. An iqa' is a drum cycle of low dum and high tak strokes, often uneven. Two examples: aqsaq in 9/8 (4+2+3) and sama'i thaqil in 10/8.

Free and fixed. A solo improvisation (taqsim, Turkish taksim) opens the mode. Composed pieces follow.

Suites. Each school strings pieces in one mode into a long program:

  • Ottoman fasıl: peşrev, semai and taksims. Composers include Gazi Giray, Dimitrie Cantemir and Şerif Çelebi.
  • Egyptian and Syrian wasla: instrumental prelude, muwashshah songs, taqsim, layali (vocal improvisation on "ya layl"), mawwal and dawr. Aleppo adds the qudud.
  • Iraqi maqam: a vocal opening (tahrir), melodic sections (qit'a), a high climax (meyana), a closing cadence (teslim) and a light song (pesta). The Persian terms for the same stages are daramad, gusheh, owj and forud.
  • Moroccan nuba (al-âla): six to seven hours in one mode, in five rhythmic sections (mizan). Twenty-four nubas once existed; eleven survive. The line runs from Baghdad (Ziryab, 9th century) to Spain to North Africa.

Ensembles. They range from one string and one drum to small orchestras. The Egyptian takht: 'ud, qanun, nay, violin and a drum. The Baghdad chalghi: santur, joza, riqq, darabuka. The Moroccan jawq is led by the rabab.

Instruments

  • Lutes: 'ud, setar, tar, tanbur, dutar, rubab
  • Zithers: qanun, santur
  • Fiddles: kamanche, rabab, violin
  • Winds: nay (flute), zurna (oboe)
  • Drums: frame drum (riqq, daf), goblet drum (darabuka, tombak), kettledrum

South Asia

Hindustani (north) · Karnatak (south)

In the art rooms: India & South Asia

The big idea

Indian classical music is improvisation held in place by two rules: raga for melody, tala for time. A drone sounds underneath. The course names four layers: drone, main melody, an accompanying melody, and percussion. There are two traditions, Hindustani in the north and Karnatak in the south.

How the music works

Raga is a set of "latent melodic possibilities." The course gives its features:

  • the basic pitches (the that or melakarta)
  • how many pitches it uses: five, six or seven
  • the emphasized pitches (vadi, samvadi)
  • its ascending and descending lines (aroha, avroha)
  • the register it favours
  • its ornaments, slides and fine tuning (gamaka, sruti)

Tala is a repeating cycle of beats (matras). The first beat is sam; claps (tali) and waves (khali) mark the rest. Common cycles: tintal, 16 beats (4+4+4+4), in the north and adi tala, 8 beats (4+2+2), in the south. Tempo runs slow, medium or fast (vilambit, madhya, drut).

The shape of a performance. It starts with an unmeasured improvisation (alap) that unfolds the raga. A composed piece follows with the drum, then improvisation on it, then often a drum solo. In the north, instrumentalists play alap–jor–jhala–gat. In the south, alapana leads to a kriti, and the showpiece is ragam–tanam–pallavi, ending with the drum solo tani avartanam.

Hindustani Karnatak

Sections sthayi, antara, sanchari pallavi, anupallavi, charanam

East Asia

China · Japan · Korea

In the art rooms: China · Japan · Korea

The big idea

In East Asia, court music is ritual and moral order. The Confucian view gives music a social and moral job, and the course puts it plainly: "idea is more important than sound." Laozi adds that "great music has fewer notes." The courts kept their ensembles almost unchanged for centuries.

China

  • The state ran music bureaus.
  • Gentry amateurs played art music; professionals played for hire.
  • The seven-string qin is the scholar's instrument: quiet, slow, and full of nature imagery.
  • Music was passed on by ear with little notation. The player acts as composer, reshaping a melody through ornament.

Japan

Gagaku, the imperial court music, has two branches:

  • kangen, for instruments: ryuteki (flute), hichiriki (oboe), shō (mouth organ), koto, biwa (lute), kakko and taiko (drums), and shōko (gong)
  • bugaku, for dance, with no strings

Noh theatre uses a flute and three drums. Japanese ensembles share the same traits:

  • a spare chamber sound
  • choreographed movement
  • instruments that imitate the voice
  • the three-part pacing jo-ha-kyū, which starts slow and ends fast

Korea

Jeongak ("right music") is the music of the ruling class. Sokak is folk music.

  • Aak is Confucian ritual music, played at the Munmyo shrine.
  • Dangak comes from Tang and Song China.
  • Hyangak is native Korean court music; Sujecheon is its best-known piece.

Court beauty is "heteronomous": the music expresses ideas and morality with restraint and moderate tempo, a "quiet beauty." Folk beauty is "autonomous" and unreserved. It grows from han, a profound sadness. The hourglass drum janggu has a yin head and a yang head, and its cycles are called jangdan, "long-short."

Southeast Asia

Cambodia · Thailand · Myanmar · Vietnam · Java · Bali · Sumatra

In the art rooms: Southeast Asia

The big idea

In Southeast Asian court music, no player is the soloist. Many parts move around one melody at different speeds, and a cycle of gongs holds them together. The course calls the ideal gotong royong, mutual help: interlocking parts, nonvirtuosic, music for "Everyman."

On the mainland the courts drew on their neighbours. The course traces the influences:

Source What it brought
India drums, stories, cymbals, dance
China zithers, pentatonic scales, flat gongs
Middle East lutes, harp, oboe, puppetry
Indonesia xylophones, knobbed gongs, layered heterophony

Myanmar leans toward India and Vietnam toward China.

The gamelan

A gamelan is an orchestra of bronze gongs and metallophones, with drums, flute, fiddle and voices. In the courts of Java and Bali it plays for ceremony, dance, and theatre: the wayang kulit shadow puppets and the Mahabharata and Ramayana stories. It uses two tunings: slendro with five tones and pelog with seven.

The Javanese gamelan works in six layers:

Layer Instruments Job
Colotomic gong ageng, kempul, kenong, ketuk the great gong ends each cycle; smaller gongs mark its parts
Drum kendang leads tempo and scans the cycle
Fixed melody saron, slentem the core melody (balungan)
Elaborating, loud bonang "melodic leader," variations
Elaborating, soft gender, siter, gambang fast variations
Vocal tone rebab, suling, singers the free, voice-like line

Bali uses the same idea at a faster pace. Pairs of metallophones (gangsa) split one line between them in fast interlocking patterns called kotekan. The two drums, female and male, interlock the same way. Bali has more than two dozen kinds of gamelan; the court kinds include gong gede and semar pegulingan.

Mainland ensembles

  • Cambodia: the pin peat ensemble, with gong circles, xylophones, oboe and drums
  • Thailand: the krajappii lute and the jakhe zither
  • Myanmar: the saung gauk, an arched harp
  • Vietnam: the nhã nhạc court orchestra of Huế and the one-string đàn bầu

In the art rooms: Africa

The big idea

In the African courts, music is the voice of the kingship: praise singers keep the ruler's genealogy, and royal drums and horns announce him. The course marks four peaks of development: West Africa (Ghana and Mali), Zimbabwe, Uganda and Mozambique. It names three kinds of musician: communal, semiprofessional (Zimbabwe) and professional, the West African griots.

How the music works

The course lists traits shared across the continent:

  • Interlocking parts. Players fit short patterns together to make one whole.
  • Density. Two things happen at once.
  • Cycles. A form repeats with variations, often as call and response.
  • Cross-rhythm. Two against three, felt against a steady inner pulse.
  • Melodic drumming and close ties between language and music.
  • Music, poetry and dance as one act.
  • "Music = model for social behavior."

The courts

  • Asante (Ghana): the fontomfrom ensemble of great drums and the kete drum ensemble, with ivory horns
  • Mande (Mali): griots with the kora, a harp-lute, and the balafon xylophone
  • Hausa (Nigeria): royal musicians of the emir's palace at Kano
  • Shona (Zimbabwe): the mbira dzavadzimu. Two players interlock a lead part (kushaura) and a following part (kutsinhira).
  • Buganda (Uganda): the amadinda log xylophone and the ennanga arched harp

Instruments

In order of how often they occur, the course lists idiophones first (mbira, xylophones, bells, rattles), then drums, then winds (flutes, horns, trumpets), then strings. The voice is shaped in many ways: pinched, buzzed, growled, yodelled.

Glossary

About fifty terms from the course

  • Aak (Korea). Confucian ritual music of the court.
  • Aerophone. An instrument sounded by vibrating air: flutes, oboes, horns, mouth organs.
  • Alap (South Asia). The unmeasured opening that unfolds a raga's pitches and mood. Alapana in the south.
  • Balungan (Java). The core melody of a gamelan piece, played on the saron and slentem.
  • Biwa (Japan). Pear-shaped lute, usually with four strings.
  • Bonang (Java). A double row of small kettle gongs in a frame; it leads the melodic variations.
  • Bugaku (Japan). The dance branch of gagaku.
  • Chordophone. An instrument sounded by strings: lutes, harps, zithers, fiddles.
  • Colotomic (Southeast Asia). The layer of gongs that marks the parts of a cycle and closes it.
  • Dastgah (Iran). A mode with its own scale, ranking of pitches and stock melodies, the base for improvisation.
  • Dhrupad (South Asia). The older, austere North Indian song form, preceded by a long alap.
  • Dum and tak (Middle East). The low and high drum strokes of a rhythmic cycle.
  • Fasıl (Turkey). The Ottoman suite in one mode: peşrev, songs, semai and taksims.
  • Gagaku (Japan). The imperial court music.
  • Gamaka (South Asia). Ornament: the slides and shakes that shape a raga's pitches.
  • Gamelan (Java, Bali). An orchestra of bronze gongs and metallophones, with drums, flute, fiddle and voices.
  • Gat (South Asia). A composed instrumental piece that anchors improvisation after the alap.
  • Gong ageng (Java). The great gong that ends each cycle.
  • Griot (West Africa). A hereditary praise singer who keeps the ruler's genealogy.
  • Heterophony. Several players sounding one melody at once, each with small variations.
  • Hichiriki (Japan). The double-reed oboe of gagaku.
  • Hyangak (Korea). Native Korean court music.
  • Idiophone. An instrument that sounds by its own body: gongs, bells, xylophones, rattles, mbira.
  • Iqa' (Middle East). A rhythmic cycle of low and high drum strokes.
  • Jangdan (Korea). A rhythmic cycle, literally "long-short."
  • Janggu (Korea). The hourglass drum, with a yin head and a yang head.
  • Jo-ha-kyū (Japan). Three-part pacing that starts slow and ends fast.
  • Kangen (Japan). The instrumental branch of gagaku.
  • Kendang (Java). The drum that leads the tempo of a gamelan.
  • Khyal (South Asia). The main North Indian vocal form: a short composition with long improvisation.
  • Kora (West Africa). The griot's harp-lute, with a gourd resonator.
  • Kotekan (Bali). Fast interlocking parts split between paired instruments.
  • Kriti (South Asia). The main South Indian song form, in three sections: pallavi, anupallavi, charanam.
  • Layali (Middle East). Vocal improvisation on the words "ya layl."
  • Maqam (Middle East). A mode: a scale with microtones, stock phrases and a mood.
  • Mbira (Southern Africa). The plucked "thumb piano"; in Zimbabwe, the mbira dzavadzimu.
  • Membranophone. An instrument sounded by a stretched skin: drums.
  • Nay (Middle East). The end-blown reed flute.
  • Noh (Japan). Masked theatre with soloists, chorus, a flute and three drums.
  • Nuba (Morocco). A suite of pieces in one mode, lasting hours; also nawba.
  • Pelog (Java). The seven-tone gamelan tuning, with some small intervals.
  • Peşrev (Turkey). The composed instrumental piece that opens a fasıl.
  • Qanun (Middle East). A plucked trapezoidal zither.
  • Qin (China). The seven-string zither of the scholar.
  • Raga (South Asia). A mode: set pitches, ranked tones, typical phrases, ornaments and mood.
  • Rebab (Java, Morocco). A bowed fiddle; in the gamelan it carries the free, voice-like line.
  • Sam (South Asia). The first beat of a tala cycle, where phrases land.
  • Santur (Iran, Iraq). A hammered trapezoidal zither.
  • Saron (Java). A bronze metallophone that plays the core melody.
  • Shō (Japan). The mouth organ of gagaku, with seventeen pipes.
  • Sitar (South Asia). The long-necked North Indian lute with movable frets.
  • Slendro (Java). The five-tone gamelan tuning, with no small intervals.
  • Tabla (South Asia). The pair of hand drums of North Indian music.
  • Takht (Egypt). The small ensemble of 'ud, qanun, nay, violin and drum.
  • Tala (South Asia). A repeating cycle of beats with a set pattern of divisions.
  • Tanpura (South Asia). The plucked lute that sounds the drone.
  • Taqsim (Middle East). A solo instrumental improvisation that opens a mode. Taksim in Turkish.
  • Tarab (Middle East). The rapt state the music aims for, the "sustained elaboration of a single mood."
  • 'Ud (Middle East). The fretless short-necked lute.
  • Wasla (Egypt, Syria). A suite in one mode: prelude, songs, improvisations.
  • Wayang kulit (Java, Bali). Shadow puppet theatre, accompanied by gamelan.
